A roofer on a ladder cannot answer the phone. A plumber under a sink cannot either. The caller does not leave a voicemail, they ring the next name on the list. For trades, where one job can be a large part of a month, a missed call is a real cost.

A missed call text-back fixes that gap with three parts: detect the missed call, reply within moments, hand the lead to you with the details already collected. This post walks through each part, using two example firms to keep it concrete. Neither is a real client; they are illustrations.

The roofing example: detect, reply, hand over

Picture Summit Ridge Roofing, an example crew of seven. The crew is on roofs most of the day, hands full, phones in pockets. A roof inquiry rings once, and if nobody answers, the caller moves down the list. Roofing jobs are large, so a single missed call can matter a lot.

Part one: detect

Your phone provider can tell the automation when a call goes unanswered. When it does, the automation creates a new lead with the caller's number and the time. It also checks whether the number is already a customer, so an existing client gets a different message than a stranger.

Part two: reply

Within moments, it sends a text along these lines: sorry, we missed you, we are up on a roof, what is the address and what is the problem?

That text is written once by you, in your words, and the automation sends it. It is not AI written on the fly, so you know exactly what customers receive. The customer's answer is captured, and if they send a photo, the link is saved with it.

Part three: handover

Now an AI step can tidy the reply into a short summary: the address, the issue, the urgency, the photo. That summary goes to you as a lead card. You call back at lunch, already knowing the job, and you decide whether it is a quote or a quick visit.

A morning at the example firm

The crew is on a roof when a call comes in and goes unanswered. Within moments, the caller gets the text. They reply that a storm took some tiles off, and send a photo of the damage. By lunchtime, the owner sees a lead card with the address, the issue and the photo, calls back, and books an inspection.

Same call, same phone, no extra staff, and fewer lost jobs.

The plumbing variant: a quote from a photo

Plumbers often get the first contact as a photo, not a call. Picture Delta Pipe and Drain, an example crew of five. Customers send photos on WhatsApp, by email, or through the website. The plumber is under a sink when they arrive, quotes get written at night from memory, and the customer has usually booked someone who replied at lunch.

The same three-part idea applies, with different names: receive, draft, approve.

Receive

Every photo and message goes into one place. The automation saves the picture, pulls out the customer's name and number, and notes what they asked for in plain words. If the photo is blurry or the question is missing, it asks one follow-up question straight away, so you never start from a vague message.

Draft

An AI step looks at the photo and the message, then builds a quote using your own price list: a call-out, the parts, the labor, in your wording. It never invents a price that is not on your list. If the job is unusual, it flags it for a site visit instead of guessing. You get a draft quote, a short note for the customer, and a reason for each line.

Approve

The draft reaches your phone. You read it, change a line if you want, and tap send. The customer gets the quote in your voice, from you, the same day. If you do nothing, nothing goes out. That is the point.

A Tuesday at the example firm

At lunchtime a customer sends a photo of a dripping pipe under a basin. The automation saves it and asks for one closer shot. The AI step drafts a quote from the price list: a call-out, a washer, and some labor. The plumber checks it between jobs, adds a line for a replacement valve, and taps send. The customer has a price before the afternoon, and books. The plumber never opened a laptop.

The mistakes to avoid

  • A long, clever first text. Keep it short, honest and human. Say you are busy, say you will call back, and ask the two questions you actually need.
  • Letting the AI guess prices. Give it your price list and one rule: if it is not on the list, flag it. A flagged job costs you a phone call. A wrong quote costs you the job.

Three things to watch

  1. Keep the first text short and honest, and do not promise a callback time you cannot keep.
  2. Check texting and call rules where you operate. This is not legal advice; check with a professional.
  3. Keep your price list current, check the first dozen quote drafts by hand, and decide what happens when the photo is not enough: a call or a visit.
