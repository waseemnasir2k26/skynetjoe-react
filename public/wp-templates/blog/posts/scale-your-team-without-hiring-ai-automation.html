When a business gets busier, the default response is to hire. Sometimes that is right. But often the new person is mostly hired to do repetitive tasks such as data entry, scheduling, follow-ups and report building, which are the jobs people tend to find least rewarding.

This article walks through how to separate the work that needs a human from the work that does not, and how to decide what to automate before you open a job listing.

What does a hire really cost before it pays off?

The salary is only part of it. Recruiting costs money or time, training takes weeks or months, and during that period the new person is learning while someone else is teaching. You also take on management time, equipment, software and the risk that the person leaves.

Then there is the question of what the person will actually do. If a large part of the role is copying data between systems, sending reminders and building reports, you are paying someone capable to do work that follows fixed patterns. People in those roles often burn out or leave, and you start again.

None of this means you should avoid hiring. It means you should know which part of the job is human and which is routine before you decide.

How the automation works, step by step

  1. List the tasks in the role. Write down every task the hire would do in a week and roughly how long each takes. Be specific, for example "enter invoices" rather than "admin".
  2. Sort them into two groups. Mark each task as routine (same steps every time) or judgement (needs creativity, empathy or negotiation). In many small business roles a large share is routine.
  3. Automate the routine group first. Take the most repetitive tasks and build automations for them: data entry from emails, scheduling, follow-ups, reports. Start with the one that costs the most hours.
  4. Keep a person on exceptions. Automations flag unusual cases to a human. That person now spends time on the cases that need judgement.
  5. Re-evaluate the hire. With the routine work covered, you may find you need a smaller role, a different role, or no new hire yet.

Use people for what people are great at, and let automation handle the repeating work.

What you need to get started

  • An honest list of what your team spends time on each week.
  • Somebody who can measure hours, even roughly, for the most repetitive tasks.
  • A willingness to document the steps of a task before automating it.
  • A plan for what your team will do with the time that comes back.

Honest limits

The video speaks of automation handling the repetitive 70% of a role. That is a framing, not a promise. The share depends entirely on the job, and some roles have far less routine work than others.

Automation is not free. Building, monitoring and updating workflows takes effort, and tools and prices change. Count that against the cost of a hire when you compare.

Be open with your team. The point is to remove tedious work, not to surprise people. Staff who understand that tend to help improve the automations rather than resist them.

Frequently asked questions

Does automation mean I should not hire at all?

No. Many roles need people and many businesses should hire. The idea is to avoid hiring someone mainly to do work that follows fixed rules, and to hire for judgement and relationships instead.

What should I automate first?

Pick the task that is repeated most often, follows clear steps and is easy to check. Common starters are data entry, scheduling, follow-ups and reporting.

What happens to my existing team?

In a good project their time shifts toward customer-facing and creative work. Talk to them early and ask which tasks they would gladly hand over.

How do I compare automation cost with a hire?

Compare the full hire cost for a year against the build and running cost of the automations, and include the time you spend maintaining them.
