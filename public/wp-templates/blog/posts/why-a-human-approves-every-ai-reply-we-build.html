There is one rule we do not bend when we build automations for customer messages: the AI can write the reply, but it never gets to send it alone.

This post explains the thinking, what is allowed to be automatic, and the two habits that go with the rule: a plan for when the automation breaks, and a test with your worst messages before it goes live.

The rule, and why we hold it

An AI is good at a first draft and at remembering the details. It can also be confidently wrong. It can sound cold when the customer is upset. It can promise something you cannot do. And when it sends the wrong message, it is your name on it, not the AI's.

Two costs, side by side

The cost of checking is small. A draft arrives on your phone, you read it in seconds, and you tap approve.

The cost of a single bad message can be a lost customer, a public complaint, or a promise you now have to keep. Put those two costs side by side and the choice is easy.

A small example

A customer writes in upset about a late delivery. An AI draft might open with a cheerful greeting and an offer of a discount nobody approved. A person reads that in two seconds, removes the discount, and adds a real apology.

That small edit is the difference between rescuing the customer and making them angrier.

Does that mean nothing is ever automatic?

No. Fixed confirmations, like "your appointment is booked", can be plain templates that you wrote, with no AI improvising. Internal alerts can be automatic too.

The line we draw is simple: if an AI is writing words to a customer, a person approves them.

Over time the drafts get better, because you fix the instructions once and every future draft improves. You keep the control, and the customer keeps a human behind the message. Customers rarely notice a good reply, but they never forget a bad one. Checking every message is how we make sure they only ever see the good ones.

Decide what happens when it breaks

Your automation will fail one day. Decide now what happens when it does.

If a step breaks, the customer should still get an answer. Route the message to a person and tell them what went wrong. One owner, one inbox, one rule. Nothing is silently dropped.

Test it with your worst messages first

Before your automation goes live, send it your worst messages:

  • An angry customer.
  • A message in another language.
  • A blank one.
  • A question it should not answer.

See what it does, fix what breaks, and decide who gets alerted when it does. Then switch it on.
