Where does the week actually go?

Most owners cannot say where their hours went. The work is fragmented into minutes: a reply here, a spreadsheet update there, a scheduling email in between. Because no single item feels big, none of them gets fixed.

Looking at four common categories is a practical way to find your own leaks. Keep a tally for a week of how many times you do each, and the biggest one will be obvious.

How the automation works, step by step

  1. Leak 1, the inbox. Hundreds of emails arrive and most do not need you. An automation reads each one, drafts a reply in your voice and flags only the messages that truly need a human. Keep a review step so nothing goes out that you have not approved.
  2. Leak 2, follow-up. A commonly repeated sales rule says many deals need around five contacts, yet most people send far fewer. An automation sends timed, personalised follow-ups and stops the moment someone replies.
  3. Leak 3, data entry. Copying details from one tool to another is slow and error-prone. An automation moves the data between tools automatically and checks it against your rules before saving.
  4. Leak 4, scheduling. The back and forth of finding a time wastes effort. An automation reads your calendar, offers genuine slots, books, reminds and reschedules.
  5. Start with the biggest leak. Do not build all four at once. Choose the one that costs you most, get it working, then move to the next.

What you need to get started

  • A one-week tally of how often each of the four leaks happens.
  • Access to your email, calendar and the tools where data lives.
  • Examples of replies you are happy with, to set your tone.
  • A clear rule for what always needs your personal attention.

Honest limits

Automations do not remove judgement from your business. They remove repetition. Anything involving money, complaints or relationships should keep a human check, especially at the start.

Each of the four needs its own setup and testing. The quality depends on your examples and rules, and it takes some weeks of review before you can trust it.

You may find that one leak is not worth automating because the volume is too low. That is a useful answer, and cheaper than building it anyway.

Frequently asked questions

Which of the four should I fix first?

The one that takes the most of your time or loses the most money. For many owners that is the inbox or follow-up, but your tally will tell you.

Can one system handle all four?

They can share the same tools, such as your email and calendar, but it is usually better to build them one at a time so each is tested properly.

Will I still need to read my email?

Yes, but far less. The aim is that you read the messages that need you and review drafts for the rest, rather than handling everything.

How do I know if it is working?

Compare your weekly tally before and after, and sample the automation's work every week. If errors appear, tighten the rules.
